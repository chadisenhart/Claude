Audited URL: foursquaremissions.com / foursquaremissions.org

NOTE ON URL: The user-provided URL (foursquaremissions.com) redirects to or
corresponds with foursquaremissions.org, which is the live indexed site with
active pages. The .com domain itself returned no indexable content in search.
If .com is a separate microsite or redirect, that split itself is worth

[A = 36-40 | B = 29-35 | C = 21-28 | D = 13-20 | F = 12 or below]

━━━ EXECUTIVE SUMMARY ━━━

FMI's strongest asset is a warm email list — people are opening because they
already care about missions. The structural problem is that the messaging
architecture was built to recruit missionaries and retain denominational
insiders, not to move a general donor to click and give. The CTAs are vague,
the giving page describes fund mechanics instead of human outcomes, and the
homepage hero targets the wrong audience entirely. The single highest-leverage
fix is rewriting the giving page with a specific anchored ask, a concrete
dollar-to-outcome statement, and one deadline — because that is the page where
warm, engaged email readers are landing and leaving without giving.

━━━ DETAILED FINDINGS ━━━

-- DIMENSION 1: HOOK STRENGTH (2/5) --

- Homepage hero headline: "Workers wanted to boldly bring the gospel of Jesus
  to every person on Earth." The org is the subject. A stranger reads this and
  learns what FMI wants, not why it should matter to them. The "workers wanted"
  framing is recruitment copy positioned as the first thing every visitor —
  including donors — encounters.

- Instagram bio (@foursquaremissions, 2,217 followers): "FMI is taking the
  whole gospel to the whole world, until all have heard." The org is the subject
  in three consecutive words. No tension. No specific person or place. A reader
  can scroll past this in under one second.

- Facebook: "No place is beyond God's reach" (found in a post). This is warmer
  and more evocative, but it is a theological platitude — there is no tension, no
  forward pull, nothing that makes you need to know what comes next.

- "See. Love. Reach." — this three-word tagline is more structurally interesting
  but functions as a slogan, not a hook. It does not open a question or name a
  tension a reader wants resolved.

- No email subject lines are publicly indexed, but the user reports decent open
  rates and near-zero click-through. This is consistent with hooks that earn an
  open (the sender's name and mission topic are enough for warm subscribers) but
  body copy that does not sustain the pull into a CTA.

- No piece of found copy opens with a named individual, a specific crisis moment,
  or a counterintuitive claim. Every opener is either the org stating its own
  purpose or a broad theological declaration.

-- DIMENSION 2: MESSAGING CLARITY (3/5) --

- The core idea — sending missionaries to people who have never heard the Gospel
  — is understandable after reading multiple pages. But it is not instantly clear
  from any single channel in five seconds.

- Four different framings compete for the primary message slot:
  (1) "Workers wanted to boldly bring the gospel of Jesus to every person on Earth"
  (2) "FMI is taking the whole gospel to the whole world, until all have heard"
  (3) "See. Love. Reach." (the giving campaign framing)
  (4) "Crossing barriers to get the gospel to unreached people and places"
  These are not contradictory, but they are not one tight message either. A donor
  who reads the email, then visits the homepage, then clicks to Give encounters
  three different framings and never hears the same sentence twice.

- Insider vocabulary runs throughout donor-facing copy: "FMI," "GMF" (Global
  Missions Fund), "unreached people groups," "indigenous movements," "Great
  Commission," "sending churches," "mobilizers." A Foursquare church member
  likely knows most of these. A donor referred by a friend, or someone who found
  FMI through social, is lost within 15 seconds.

- The Global Missions Fund — apparently FMI's primary giving vehicle — is
  described as: "supports key regional leaders worldwide, allowing them to direct
  their full attention to expansion of church movements, partnership toward
  indigenous leadership, and the needs of nations with unreached people and
  places." This sentence describes an organizational structure. It does not
  describe a human outcome.

- The connection between FMI and The Foursquare Church denomination is unclear
  to outsiders. Is FMI independent? Is it a department of the denomination? Does
  giving to FMI go to the same place as giving to a local Foursquare church? The
  answer probably exists somewhere on the site; it is not surfaced clearly in any
  donor-facing copy.

-- DIMENSION 3: CTA EFFECTIVENESS (2/5) --

- The primary giving CTA found indexed: "Give now to FMI's Global Missions Fund."
  No amount anchored. No deadline stated. No concrete outcome tied to the gift
  (no "$50 trains a local leader" or "$25/month funds one month of field work").
  A motivated donor clicks through and has no idea what a meaningful gift looks
  like.

- Multiple competing giving destinations exist: foursquaremissions.org/give/,
  give.foursquare.org/give/f4318499/, give.foursquare.org/campaign/
  foursquare-missions-international/c454031/, and the "See. Love. Reach."
  campaign page. A donor clicking from email to "give" may land on any of these
  and encounter a different page with different framing. Splitting the ask splits
  the conversion.

- The site's navigation presents four parallel primary actions: Give, Go, Pray,
  Send. These are not sequenced or prioritized for a donor. A first-time visitor
  with money to give has to self-select their path from four equally-weighted
  options.

- No urgency mechanism is visible in any indexed giving copy: no match deadline,
  no campaign goal thermometer, no "X days left," no "we're $12,000 short of our
  goal." Without a reason to act today, a warm reader who intended to give can
  close the tab and never return.

- The "Wanted" page CTA for missionary recruiters: "Fill out this form, and an
  FMI representative will reach out." Generic. No clear next step. No
  differentiation between the form a potential missionary fills out and the form
  a "mobilizer" (pastor) fills out.

-- DIMENSION 4: PSYCHOLOGICAL LEVERS (2/5) --

Levers in use:

  Authority: USED — "Since 1924," 100-year history, 158 nations/territories,
  100,000+ churches in partnership, 300+ missionary workers. This is the
  dominant lever and it is deployed consistently.

  Story: PARTIAL — "See. Love. Reach." has narrative structure and the copy
  references "unreached people groups in closed nations." But story requires a
  named individual with a specific before/after arc. No named person appears in
  any indexed FMI donor-facing copy. Collective story ("unreached people groups")
  is not the same thing as a story.

  Urgency: NOT USED — No deadline, no match expiration, no campaign close date
  in any indexed material. "Until all have heard" expresses eternal urgency but
  not actionable urgency.

  Scarcity: NOT USED — No "only X spots remain," no "this match ends Friday,"
  no limited-time framing of any kind.

  Social Proof: NOT USED — FMI has 13,288 Facebook followers and a network of
  100,000+ churches. Neither of these is deployed as social proof for donors.
  "Join 13,000 people who support FMI" or "4,200 donors gave last year to reach
  closed nations" — neither framing appears in indexed copy. Organizational
  scale is cited (authority), but donor community size is not.

  Reciprocity: NOT USED — Prayer guides exist on the site, but there is no
  evidence of a free resource delivered before the ask. No field-report PDF, no
  "here's what's happening in one village right now" email before the donate
  ask. The relationship goes directly to the ask.

  Identity: NOT USED — No "you are the kind of person who..." framing. No copy
  that positions giving to FMI as an expression of who the donor already
  believes themselves to be.

  Belonging: NOT USED — "Foursquare family" language appears in broader
  Foursquare church contexts but is not leveraged in FMI donor copy. Donors are
  not invited into a named community of supporters. There is no insider identity
  for regular FMI donors equivalent to "FMI Partner" or similar.

Levers being left on the table:

  Social Proof — FMI has the numbers to use this (100,000 churches, 300+
  workers, years of history). Reformatting organizational scale into donor
  community proof ("join 8,000 monthly givers") would lower the decision barrier
  for fence-sitters.

  Urgency — The single fastest mechanism to lift click-to-give conversion from
  email. Every send should include a reason to act before the next send arrives.

  Identity — Faith-based donors often give because of who they believe God has
  called them to be, not just what the org does. "People like you don't just
  pray for the unreached — they act" is a different ask than "give to the GMF."

  Belonging — FMI's best donors are probably deeply embedded in the Foursquare
  church community. A named inner circle ("FMI Partners," "Frontline Givers")
  with exclusive updates from the field would deepen retention and upgrade
  giving.

  Reciprocity — A prayer guide, a quarterly field letter from a specific
  missionary, a "here's one thing happening this week in Southeast Asia" email
  — all of these give before asking and build the trust that turns opens
  into clicks.

-- DIMENSION 5: AUDIENCE PRECISION (2/5) --

- The homepage hero — "Workers wanted" — is explicitly addressed to potential
  missionaries. Donors who land on the homepage see recruiting copy. This is the
  most concrete symptom of the audience confusion: the person FMI needs to click
  a CTA is reading copy meant for someone who wants to move overseas.

- The site appears to serve at minimum four distinct audiences simultaneously:
  potential missionaries (Go, Wanted, Long-Term, Short-Term pages), current
  denominational donors (Give, GMF), church pastors (Sending, Mobilizers
  section), and prayer partners (Pray page). No segmented entry points are
  visible. All four land on the same homepage.

- "Everyone can make a difference" is not present verbatim, but the structure
  of the site — "You can Give, Go, Pray, or Send" — functions identically. It
  addresses four types of engagement simultaneously rather than leading any one
  type of person down a clear path.

- The copy assumes the reader already cares about the Great Commission. The
  "Why Missions?" page exists, which suggests FMI knows it needs to earn that
  caring — but it is a secondary page, not the entry point. A first-time visitor
  from a social share who is spiritually curious but not already missions-
  committed hits the "Workers wanted" hero and either self-selects into the
  missionary path (small pool) or leaves (everyone else).

- No segmentation between cold audience and warm donors is visible. The person
  who subscribed to the email list last week and the 10-year monthly donor get
  the same message.

-- DIMENSION 6: VOICE CONSISTENCY (3/5) --

- The faith vocabulary is consistent across channels: "gospel," "nations,"
  "unreached," "disciples," "Great Commission." This is the skeleton of a voice.

- But the register shifts considerably depending on where you look:
  - Instagram bio: punchy, slogan-tight ("taking the whole gospel to the whole
    world, until all have heard")
  - Homepage hero: slightly formal, recruitment-framed ("Workers wanted to
    boldly bring the gospel of Jesus to every person on Earth")
  - GMF giving page: bureaucratic, committee-memo register ("supports key
    regional leaders worldwide, allowing them to direct their full attention to
    expansion of church movements, partnership toward indigenous leadership")
  - "See. Love. Reach." campaign: aspirational, poetic ("God sees the spiritually
    lost and invites us to see the world as He does: with compassion for the
    lost and courage to act")
  - "Wanted" page: warmer, conversational ("courageous couples, brave
    individuals, skilled workers and daring families who are unafraid to follow
    God's call, no matter the cost")

- A donor who receives an email (likely written in one register), clicks to the
  giving page (bureaucratic GMF copy), and then checks Instagram (punchy bio)
  would not immediately recognize these as one organization's voice.

- The shift between "aspirational missions voice" and "organizational
  administrative voice" is the most damaging inconsistency — because the
  administrative voice shows up exactly where the donor is making a decision.

-- DIMENSION 7: CHANNEL COVERAGE (3/5) --

- Website: Active, multiple pages, appears functional. Mobile and SEO status
  cannot be confirmed from available data.

- Email: Active. User reports decent open rates, near-zero click-through. List
  exists and is warm. The channel is working at the awareness layer and failing
  at the conversion layer.

- Facebook: 13,288 likes / 232 currently talking = ~1.7% engagement rate. For
  context, average Facebook page engagement is 1-2%. FMI is at the floor of
  acceptable, not above it. The community is large enough to leverage but is
  not being activated.

- Instagram: @foursquaremissions — 2,217 followers, 585 posts. The post volume
  (585) is high relative to the follower count. Volume is not compensating for
  reach. No evidence of a growth strategy visible in indexed data.

- Video: Referenced in foursquare.org resources ("Go with Foursquare Missions
  International" video, "Give with FMI" video), but whether these are actively
  promoted or primarily exist as reference content is unclear.

- Short-form video (Reels, Shorts): No evidence of an active short-form video
  presence. If FMI's target donor is under 50, this is a meaningful gap.

- Direct mail / print: Not evident in any indexed materials. Church kits and
  connection offering materials exist (Foursquare Connection 2026 in San Antonio
  referenced a missions offering), but these appear to be denominational
  distribution, not FMI-controlled direct mail.

- Cross-channel path: Social posts appear to link to the website; the website
  collects email subscribers; email sends drives (in theory) to giving pages.
  The architecture exists but the conversion layer (email to give) is failing,
  which suggests the problem is in the ask mechanics, not the channel structure.

-- DIMENSION 8: STRATEGIC BLIND SPOTS (2/5) --

Blind spot 1 — The missionary/donor channel collision:
FMI's highest-traffic entry point (homepage) is optimized for missionary
recruitment. The homepage hero is a job posting. The "Wanted," "Go," and
sending-church pages reflect significant investment. This is appropriate if the
top priority is deploying missionaries. It is the wrong funnel if the problem
is donor click-through. The org may not have consciously decided which audience
owns the homepage, and so it serves neither audience well.

Blind spot 2 — Organizational metrics masquerading as donor impact:
"158 nations," "100,000 churches," "300+ workers," "since 1924" — these are
authority signals, not impact statements. They tell a donor how big FMI is, not
what a donor's gift does. There is a consistent pattern across all channels of
leading with organizational scale rather than human outcome. A donor asking
"what does my $100 do?" cannot answer that question from any indexed FMI copy.

Blind spot 3 — Treating opens as a conversion signal:
Decent email open rates feel like success. They are not. Open rates measure
subject line performance and sender trust — both of which may be working fine.
The problem is in the email body and the destination page. An org that sees
good opens and attributes the lack of clicks to "messaging not landing" is
looking at the symptom (clicks) and diagnosing the wrong component (messaging
generally). The specific failure is: no specific ask, no deadline, and a
giving page that cannot answer "why now?" or "what exactly will this do?"

Levers being left on the table:

  Social Proof — FMI has a large network (100,000 partner churches, 13,000+
  Facebook followers). Reframing this as donor community size ("join 8,000
  people who give monthly to reach the unreached") changes a statistic into
  an invitation.

  Urgency — The fastest mechanism to lift email-to-give conversion. Quarterly
  matched giving windows, campaign goal thermometers, or end-of-year deadlines
  give warm readers a reason to click today rather than next week (which means
  never).

  Scarcity — Limited GO Team slots, missionary deployment windows closing,
  or a giving match with a hard cap — all of these create real scarcity that
  already exists in FMI's programs. Naming it explicitly is not manipulation;
  it is honest communication about how missions deployment works.

  Identity — Faith donors give from identity ("I am someone who believes the
  unreached deserve to hear the Gospel, and that means I have to do something").
  No copy addresses the donor's sense of who they are or who they want to be.

  Belonging — Regular, committed donors to a missions org want to feel like
  insiders. A named community tier, exclusive field updates, and language that
  separates "our people" from the general public would increase both retention
  and upgrade giving.

  Reciprocity — Deliver something of value before the ask: a quarterly printed
  field letter from a named missionary, a prayer guide tied to a specific
  unreached people group, a short video of life in a village FMI serves.
  Reciprocity lowers the psychological barrier between reading and clicking.

━━━ PRIORITY FIX LIST ━━━

Ranked by estimated impact (high impact + low effort at the top):

1. Rewrite the giving page with a specific anchored ask and one deadline.
   This is the direct cause of "nobody clicks through to give." The current
   giving page describes fund mechanics, not outcomes. Replace "supports key
   regional leaders worldwide, allowing them to direct their full attention to
   expansion of church movements" with something like: "Your gift of $75 funds
   one month of field expenses for a missionary family in a closed nation."
   Add a deadline or a match. Give donors a reason to act before closing the
   tab. This is the highest-leverage change in the audit because it is the
   page where warm, engaged email readers arrive and leave without converting.
   Cost to fix: one afternoon of copy revision. Cost to ignore: every email
   that earns an open but no gift.

2. Add urgency to every email CTA.
   Decent open rates with near-zero click-through means the audience is warm
   but the ask is not doing its job. Every email should include at minimum one
   of: a deadline ("match expires Sunday"), a campaign goal ("we're $4,200
   from our goal"), or a time-sensitive story ("a GO Team deploys in 11 days
   and needs $3,000 more to go"). The subject line earns the open; urgency in
   the body earns the click. Without it, every send is an announcement that the
   reader can admire and close.

3. Create a separate donor homepage entry point (or a donor-specific email
   landing page).
   The current homepage hero recruits missionaries. The email list is
   (presumably) donors. These audiences need different entry experiences. At
   minimum, every fundraising email should link to a dedicated landing page —
   not the general homepage — with one message, one story, one ask, one button.
   No navigation. No competing paths. This can be built as a simple campaign
   page and will outperform the general homepage for donation conversion
   immediately.

4. Add one named individual story to every major donor touchpoint.
   The largest psychological lever FMI is leaving unused is specific story.
   "Unreached people groups in closed nations" is a category. A named person
   in a named country with a before/after moment is a story. Story does not
   require a case study — one paragraph with a name, a country, and a single
   moment of change is enough to shift the emotional register of a giving ask.
   This costs one interview with one field worker per quarter.

5. Strip insider language from donor-facing copy.
   "GMF," "FMI," "indigenous movements," "unreached people groups," "Great
   Commission," "mobilizer," "sending church" — audit every piece of donor-
   facing copy for these terms and replace each one with plain language.
   "Indigenous movements" = "local church networks led by local people."
   "Unreached people groups" = "communities who have never heard the Gospel."
   The Foursquare insider reading this already knows what these terms mean; the
   Foursquare adjacent donor who gave once in 2021 and is now reading an email
   does not. Write for the latter.

6. Deploy social proof on the giving page and in emails.
   FMI has the numbers: 13,000+ Facebook followers, 100,000 partner churches,
   100 years of presence. These are organizational authority stats. Convert them
   into donor community proof: "4,800 people gave to FMI last year to reach
   closed nations. Add your name." This is a one-line copy change on the giving
   page and a one-line addition to each email. It changes the ask from
   institutional ("support this org") to communal ("join these people").

7. Consolidate giving destinations to one URL.
   Three or more active giving URLs (foursquaremissions.org/give, two
   give.foursquare.org pages, the See Love Reach campaign) split traffic,
   split reporting, and create a disjointed donor experience. Choose one
   canonical giving URL, redirect everything else to it, and make that page
   the only destination emails point to. This also consolidates tracking so
   you can actually see what is working.

8. Establish a pre-ask reciprocity touchpoint in email.
   For new email subscribers and lapsed donors, build a short welcome or re-
   engagement sequence that delivers value before the first ask: a field story,
   a prayer guide for a specific region, a short video from a missionary.
   FMI's give page notes prayer guides exist — deploy them as the first thing
   a new subscriber receives, not as a buried site resource. Three emails of
   value before the first direct ask would likely lift click-through rates on
   subsequent giving emails measurably.

━━━ STRATEGIC DIAGNOSIS ━━━

The root cause connecting every weak score in this audit is an audience
identity crisis that has been baked into FMI's messaging architecture. The
org built its digital presence to serve three audiences at once — potential
missionaries, denominational donors, and church pastors considering sending
partnerships — without clearly deciding which audience owns which channel or
which page. The result is a homepage hero that recruits workers (targeting a
small pool), a giving page that describes organizational structures (targeting
no one specifically), and an email list of warm supporters who open every send
and click nothing. The messaging is not the problem. The architecture is.

The email open rates are the most important data point in this audit. They
tell you the audience is there, they trust the sender, and they are willing
to give their attention. The near-zero click-through tells you the ask is not
earning action — not because the cause is unappealing, but because the ask
never gives a specific person a specific reason to act right now. Fixing that
one conversion layer — from email body to giving page — with a specific dollar
amount, a named human story, an urgency mechanism, and a single CTA does not
require a rebrand. It requires a clear-eyed decision about who FMI is talking
to when it asks for money, followed by copy that actually speaks to that person.
Everything else in this fix list is secondary to that decision.
