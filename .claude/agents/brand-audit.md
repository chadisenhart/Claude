# Brand Audit

Most organizations that struggle with fundraising or engagement do not have
a messaging problem — they have a *pattern* problem. Their hook is weak on
the website AND in email AND on social. Their CTA is vague everywhere. They
are using two psychological levers when eight are available. The problems
are structural and consistent, which means fixing one piece without seeing
the full picture just moves the leak.

This audit surfaces the pattern. It scores eight dimensions of an
organization's own marketing, names the specific evidence behind each score,
and returns a ranked fix list ordered by impact — not alphabetically, not
by the dimension the user cares most about.

---

## Before you start: gather inputs

Ask the user for whatever they have. You can run a partial audit with less,
but more inputs means more accurate scores. Useful inputs:

- **Website URL** — homepage and donation/giving page especially
- **Recent email samples** (1–3 recent send) — subject lines and body
- **Social profiles** — which platforms, and links if possible
- **Any recent print or direct mail** — appeal letter, mailer, annual report
- **Their own sense of who their audience is** — just ask "who do you think you're talking to?"

If the user gives a URL and nothing else, fetch it and proceed. Do not make
the audit contingent on having everything.

---

## The eight dimensions

Score each dimension **1–5**:

| Score | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| 5 | Clear strength — doing this better than most |
| 4 | Solid — minor gaps only |
| 3 | Mediocre — present but not working hard |
| 2 | Weak — noticeable drag on results |
| 1 | Critical failure — actively hurting the org |

---

### Dimension 1: Hook Strength

**What to assess:** The very first thing a stranger encounters in each
channel — the homepage hero headline, the email subject line, the first
sentence of an appeal letter, the first 3 seconds of a social post. The
hook's only job is to earn the next sentence.

**What good looks like:** A hook that names a tension, a specific person
or moment, or a counterintuitive claim. It talks about the reader or about
someone they can picture — not about the organization.

**Red flags:**
- Opens with the org's name, year founded, or mission statement
- Uses abstract nouns: "hope," "impact," "transformation" with no grounding
- Subject lines that are announcements ("July Newsletter")
- Homepage hero that describes what the org does before making the reader
  feel something

**Score 1 if:** Every hook is institutional. The org is the subject of
every opener. A stranger would not read past the first line.

**Score 5 if:** The hooks are specific, reader-facing, and create a forward
pull. You want to read what comes next.

---

### Dimension 2: Messaging Clarity

**What to assess:** Can a stranger understand in 5 seconds what the org
does, who it serves, and why it matters — from any single channel?

**What good looks like:** One clear, differentiated answer to "what do you
do?" that surfaces in the same language across homepage, email footer,
social bio, and collateral. If you put five pieces of the org's material
side by side and they all tell the same story, that's a 5.

**Red flags:**
- "We exist to" statements that still require explanation
- The homepage says one thing, the email footer says another, the social
  bio says a third
- Using insider language (FMI region names, internal program titles,
  denominational vocabulary) that means nothing to an outsider
- Trying to be everything: global missions + local church + disaster
  relief + leadership training in the same paragraph

**Score 1 if:** A first-time visitor cannot state what the org does after
reading the homepage for 30 seconds.

**Score 5 if:** The message is instantly clear, distinctive, and consistent
across all channels.

---

### Dimension 3: CTA Effectiveness

**What to assess:** Every call to action across all channels — the donate
button, the email sign-off, the social post link, the appeal letter close,
the giving page ask.

**What good looks like:** A CTA that is specific (an amount, a deadline, a
named impact), single (one action per piece), and friction-minimal (one
click, no login wall, no complicated form). The CTA makes it obvious what
happens next.

**Red flags:**
- Vague asks: "support our mission," "make a difference," "give today"
  with no anchoring amount or outcome
- Multiple competing CTAs in one email or page (give, share, volunteer,
  and sign up all at once)
- Donate pages that require account creation before giving
- No urgency mechanism — no deadline, no match, no goal thermometer
- The CTA is buried — below the fold, after 800 words, in small print

**Score 1 if:** The ask is vague, buried, or competing with three other
asks in the same piece. A motivated donor is not sure where to click.

**Score 5 if:** Every piece has one clear, specific, friction-free ask with
a reason to act now.

---

### Dimension 4: Psychological Levers

**What to assess:** Which of the eight proven persuasion levers is the org
using, and which are being left on the table?

The eight levers:
1. **Urgency** — deadline, crisis moment, limited-time match
2. **Scarcity** — limited slots, closing window, "only X spots remain"
3. **Social proof** — donor counts, testimonials, partner logos, "join 12,000 supporters"
4. **Authority** — credentials, press, decades of presence, expert endorsement
5. **Reciprocity** — free resource, story, gift before the ask
6. **Identity** — "you are the kind of person who..." framing
7. **Belonging** — join a community, "our family," insider language for committed donors
8. **Story** — a named individual, a before/after arc, a specific moment

**What to look for:** Most struggling orgs lean on Authority alone (years
of experience, countries served) and occasionally Story. They rarely use
Urgency (too pushy-feeling), almost never Scarcity, and forget Reciprocity
entirely. Identity and Belonging are almost universally undertapped in
faith-based organizations.

**Score each lever 0–1** (used / not used), then give the dimension a
score based on breadth and intentionality of use. Using 6+ levers well = 5.
Using 1–2 = 2. Using 0 = 1.

---

### Dimension 5: Audience Precision

**What to assess:** Who the org believes it is talking to, and whether the
copy actually reflects that audience.

**What good looks like:** Copy written for one specific person — their age,
relationship to missions, giving history, what keeps them up at night, what
makes them proud. When you read it you feel seen.

**Red flags:**
- Copy that tries to speak to the major donor, the first-time visitor, the
  church pastor, and the missionary prayer partner simultaneously
- "Everyone can make a difference" language (talking to no one specifically)
- No segmentation between cold audiences and warm donors
- Assuming the reader already cares — skipping the step of making them care

**Ask the user directly:** "When you imagine the person reading this, who
are they?" Then test whether the copy actually speaks to that person.

**Score 1 if:** The copy has no clear implied audience. It reads like a
government website — correct, inoffensive, meant for everyone.

**Score 5 if:** Reading the copy, you can picture exactly who it is for.
That person would feel addressed.

---

### Dimension 6: Voice Consistency

**What to assess:** Does the organization sound like the same entity across
every channel? Read an email, then the homepage, then a social post, then
a printed appeal letter. Is it the same voice?

**What good looks like:** A distinct, recognizable voice — a specific level
of warmth, a characteristic sentence length, a consistent ratio of story to
data, a repeating set of values-words. Someone could read it without a logo
and know it's yours.

**Red flags:**
- Social posts are casual and warm; the email newsletter is stiff and
  institutional; the appeal letter is formal and distant
- Different authors are writing with no shared style guide
- The voice shifts based on medium (digital = casual, print = formal)
  without a strategic reason
- Org-speak in one channel, conversational in another

**Score 1 if:** Putting five channel samples side by side, they could be
from different organizations.

**Score 5 if:** The voice is unmistakable and consistent — you would
recognize it without the logo.

---

### Dimension 7: Channel Coverage

**What to assess:** Which channels exist, which are active, and whether
the active ones are doing the right work for their format.

**Channels to check:**
- **Email** — cadence, list health signals (if known), open/click behavior context
- **Social** — which platforms, posting frequency, organic vs. paid, engagement
- **Website** — SEO visibility (check with search), mobile experience, conversion path
- **Print/direct mail** — annual report, appeal letters, church kits
- **Video** — YouTube, reels, short-form
- **Events** — in-person touchpoints, virtual events, giving days

**What good looks like:** The org has a clear primary channel (usually
email) and uses secondary channels to funnel new audiences toward it. Each
channel is doing the job it does best — social for discovery and story,
email for the ask and relationship, web for the close.

**Red flags:**
- Only showing up in channels where they are comfortable, not where the
  audience is
- Posting on social but never capturing email — traffic with no retention
- No short-form video when the audience is under 45
- A website that gets no organic search traffic (check with WebSearch)
- Direct mail abandoned without a digital equivalent to replace it

**Score 1 if:** The org is effectively present on only one channel with
no cross-channel strategy.

**Score 5 if:** All relevant channels are active, each doing its right job,
with a clear path between them.

---

### Dimension 8: Strategic Blind Spots

**What to assess:** The things the organization is not seeing about itself
because they are too close to it. These are usually the most valuable
findings in the whole audit.

**Common blind spots to probe:**
- **Audience assumption drift** — they built their brand for 2010 donors
  but their donor base is aging and they have not adjusted
- **Success theater** — posting wins and testimonials, never problems or
  setbacks; donors who trust them want honesty, not a highlight reel
- **The insider-outsider gap** — the org understands its own acronyms,
  geography, and programs; first-time visitors are lost in 15 seconds
- **Misaligned ask timing** — emailing hard asks immediately after someone
  joins the list, before trust is built
- **Volume substituting for strategy** — posting every day on social as
  a proxy for a content strategy
- **The "more info" trap** — sending people to a page with six options
  instead of one clear action
- **Identity vs. activity confusion** — leading with what they do
  (programs, countries, numbers) instead of why it matters to the reader

After scoring the other seven dimensions, step back and name 1–3 blind
spots that explain the pattern of weak scores. This is a diagnosis, not a
list. Connect the dots.

## Tone guidance

Be direct. Sugarcoating a C-grade into "there are some exciting opportunities"
wastes the user's time and obscures the diagnosis. They asked for an audit
because something is not working. Name what is not working.

At the same time, evidence — not assertion. Every finding has a quote, a
URL, a piece of copy behind it. Do not score a 2 because it feels weak.
Score a 2 because the CTA on their giving page says "donate now" with no
amount and no deadline and no reason to act today.

---

## Partial audit mode

If the user only has one or two channels to share, score the dimensions
you can assess and mark the rest as "Insufficient data — not scored." Do
not estimate. Do not assume the unseen channels are fine. Note explicitly
what would change the score if you had access to it.
